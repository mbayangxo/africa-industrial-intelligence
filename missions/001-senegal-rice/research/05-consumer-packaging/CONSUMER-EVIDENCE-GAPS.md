# Consumer evidence gaps

## Critical gaps

| Gap | Why it matters | Desk evidence to seek | Later field evidence if unresolved | Decision blocked |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Choice set by location and channel | A buyer cannot choose an unavailable product | Retail/market audits, distribution studies, household expenditure microdata with product detail | Structured outlet observation and buyer intercepts | Distribution and assortment strategy |
| Actual versus stated choice | Reported preference may not predict purchase | Transaction, scanner, diary or incentive-compatible experiment evidence | Choice experiment tied to realistic products and prices | Demand and revenue assumptions |
| Product definitions | “Local” and “imported” may hide different grain types, processing and lots | Study protocols, standards, product measurements | Blind coded sample assessment | Any origin-based conclusion |
| Dish/use occasion | Desired cooking properties may differ by preparation | Culinary/consumer studies reporting named uses | Household and food-service preparation tests | Product specification |
| Grain and brokenness preference | Aggregate labels can conceal distinct segments | Disaggregated consumption and choice research | Blind tests across controlled grain specifications | Milling and grading configuration |
| Sensory and cooking performance | Appearance alone does not establish eating quality | Instrumental and sensory research with methods | Standardized cooking and sensory protocol | Quality targets |
| Cleanliness and consistency | Perception and measured defect rates are different constructs | Lot-level grading/testing plus consumer studies | Laboratory grading and repeated-lot comparison | Quality-control requirements |
| Price sensitivity | Affordability can dominate attribute preferences | Date/location-specific prices and credible demand studies | Repeated price collection and incentive-compatible choices | Pricing and pack architecture |
| Pack size and cash flow | Unit value and affordable transaction size can point in different directions | Pack-level price/volume evidence | Outlet audit and purchasing diaries | Pack-size portfolio |
| Packaging utility | Durability, storage and resealing needs are unverified | Packaging-use studies and complaint data | In-home use test | Package format |
| Branding and trust | Brand, origin, certification and seller trust can be confounded | Blind versus disclosed experiments | Randomized information/pack treatments | Brand investment |
| Food-service demand | Restaurant procurement may differ from household purchasing | Food-service studies or procurement records | Cook/restaurant interviews and kitchen trials | B2B offer |
| Urban/rural and subnational differences | A single-city result cannot represent Senegal | Subnational studies and microdata | Stratified multisite research | National generalization |
| Temporal stability | Older studies may not reflect current supply, prices or brands | Comparable studies across periods | Current benchmark study | Current market interpretation |
| Distribution constraints | Sales may reflect stock and route economics rather than preference | Stock-out, wholesaler and route evidence | Longitudinal outlet observation | Go-to-market model |
| Trust signals and standards | Consumers may interpret labels differently from regulators | Label rules and comprehension research | Comprehension testing | Claims and label design |

## Evidence needed before commercial conclusions

A commercial conclusion should not proceed until the evidence can separate intrinsic product attributes from packaging/information, price, and availability. At minimum, decision-relevant evidence should define the compared products, sampled population, place and period; reveal the choice context; and expose material uncertainty. Rare evidence may still be retained at lower confidence with an explicit verification plan.

## Questions desk research may be unable to settle

* Current product and pack availability by neighborhood and channel.
* Current transaction prices, promotions, stock-outs and credit practices.
* Whether sensory performance differs across specific current lots.
* How buyers trade off quality, price, pack size, brand, seller trust and availability under realistic constraints.
* Current household and food-service cooking performance.
* Interpretation of proposed names, visuals or origin signals.
* Packaging durability in actual transport, storage and reuse conditions.

These should become field hypotheses only after existing Senegal evidence and methods have been reviewed. Their presence here does not authorize data collection.
